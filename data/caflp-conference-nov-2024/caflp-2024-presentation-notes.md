# Food Waste, Property, Disorder and the Police Power


## Introduction/Agenda

The context for my talk today is the question of how local actors participate in the governance of food loss and waste. What is unique or uniquely interesting about food waste governance at the local level as compared to other policy domains familiar to local government and their partners?

My answer to this question is going to turn to a little known and poorly understood area of Canadian government authority and governmentality which, in the US where it is widely employed, would be called the "police power".  

The "police power" is not, as the name would suggest, a power about municipal policing or police officers at all. The "police power" refers to a specific *mode* or *form* of governance that differs in several important ways from modernist forms of regulatory governance which have come to be taken more or less for granted in contemporary food systems and beyond. 

The police power is fundamentally concerned with property, with public order and disorder--including moral order and disorder--and with good governance and community welfare. Each of these dimensions, I think, have something big to tell us about law's capacity to address food waste. 

For the remainder of this talk I'm going to do three things:

1. Describe the police power in Canada
2. Connect food waste in the "circular economy" to food waste as property and public disorder
3. Argue that we need the police power concept to understand how local actors regulate food waste as collaborative governance 

## What is the Police Power?

As with so much in Anglo-Canadian law, the origins of the idea of police can be traced back to Blackstone's mid-eighteenth century *Commentaries*:

> By the public police and oeconomy I mean the due regulation and domestic order of the kingdom: whereby the individuals of the state, like members of a well-governed family, are bound to conform their general behaviour to the rules of propriety, good  neighbourhood, and good manners; and to be decent, industrious, and inoffensive in their respective stations.

A few features of Blackstone's definition are worth noting:

- It links "police" with "economy", which as Markus Dubber has pointed out, traces all the way back to early Greek writing on economics in its literal meaning of "government of the household for the common good of the whole family" [@dubber2005police, 1280-1]. 

- This conception of police as patriarchal, internal governance of the family is in turn linked closely to ideas of "order" (including moral order).

- The governance of the economy as well-organized family turns heavily on the formulation and application of *discretion* by the head of the household, who holds plenary authority over the family to ensure its welfare 

These ideas were picked up enthusiastically by the early architects of the American constitutional order an later the American courts, who came associate a wide range of government actions with state governance as "household management". This idea became particularly important for local governments, where the types of regulatory concerns and the practice of governance found an easy fit with the goal of a well-ordered municipal household.

In Canada, and in Britain before it, Blackstone's formulation of police received a much more cautious reception. As Mariana Valverde has observed, one can search the law books in vain for any reference to "police power"--though she also notes that it is exactly this void that makes the idea such a good one to think with [@valverde2006PeaceOrderGood, 73-75].

Nevertheless, the police power has been and, arguably, remains, a feature of Canadian law. 

In *Hodge v the Queen* (1883), the JCPC affirmed that the provinces in Canada may exercise the police power as an aspect of its jurisdiction over "property and civil rights"--a jurisdiction which is not infrequently delegated to local governments. 

In *Hodge*, the defendant tavern owner was convicted of permitting use of his billiard table after 7pm--an after-hours activity that was prohibited by the local liquor licence commissioners (a municipal body delegated its powers by the province). Hodge challenged the prohibition on billiard playing as *ultra vires* the commissioners but the law lords disagreed, finding that the commissioners were within the scope of their powers to regulate the social disorders of alcohol *and* any of its associated activities, of which billiards were one: 

> Their Lordships consider that the powers intended to be conferred by the Act in question, when properly understood, are to make regulations in the nature of police or municipal regulations of a merely local character for the good government of taverns, etc., licensed for the sale of liquors by retail, and such as are calculated to preserve, in the municipality, peace and public decency, and repress drunkenness and disorderly and riotous conduct. As such they cannot be said to interfere with the general regulation of trade and commerce which belongs to the Dominion Parliament [...]

Following *Hodge*, the nature and scope of the police power in Canada therefore became intimately linked to the local regulation of property, particularly where that regulation was pursue for the purposes of social order. 

However the further development of "police-like powers" in the Canadian context quickly shifted from a constitutional matter to one of interpreting the delegation of statutory authority. The focus here was on "omnibus" or "general welfare" powers that found their way into municipal empowering statutes and whose language endowing local governments with broad powers to pass by-laws for "health, safety, morality and welfare of the inhabitants of the municipality in matters not specifically provided for" elsewhere in the legislation reflected both the language and the spirit of police powers as they had come to be understood by the U.S. courts [@stein1971municipal, 543-4].

In *Re Morrison and City of Kingston* (1938) this "generous" interpretation of municipal omnibus clauses was severely limited and rendered them effectively inoperative for much of the twentieth century, until both judicial and legislative interest was revived following *Shell v Vancouver (City* in the early 1990s and later in *Spraytech*. 

The current situation is a certain tension between (1) a return by the courts to a more open approach to police powers as captured by statutory "general welfare" clauses and (2) a spheres-of-jurisdiction approach found in many contemporary provincial municipal acts, which grant some narrower flexibility removing much of the function reliance on the general welfare clauses. 

## Property, Disorder and Circular Economy

How then is the "police power" related to municipal governance of food waste? This question brings us to topic of the "circular economy" and its implications.

The circular economy is an attempt to transition away from linear economic processes and to reconceive "waste" as resource--a valued and valuable "thing" that is both produced by and will serve as an input into linked sites of economic production. 

For food "waste", this means primarily re-envisioning unused food as both a private and public asset that can be either composted for re-integration into the agricultural production or other soil-based uses, sometimes used for animal food, or collected for biogas production that can drive transportation networks, such as by fuelling public transit. 

A consequence of the circular economy model noticed by some scholars is that, in seeking to transform waste into a valuable resource it simultaneously opens the door to--and in some ways necessitates--the application of property regimes. To the extent that food waste in conventional models, once thrown in the bin, was abandoned and considered value-less (or worse, a net cost) it was largely invisible to established property institutions. But once such "waste" acquires value it becomes a natural object of property rights.

[Application of police power]

Not only does its newfound status as property bring food waste within the possible ambit of police, the imperative for it to maintain that status itself becomes a matter of public order. As @[holmberg2021circular, 348] observe, sustaining the smooth circular economy envisioned by advocates places a heavy reliance on "make-up work"--the constant "tidying" required to prevent "leaks" out of the system and to perpetuate the neatness on which circularity is constructed. Supporting and regulating this good order of the community is a central concern of and justification for the police power. In this sense, circularity takes on a certain moral economy as well [@lohnes2021regulating].
